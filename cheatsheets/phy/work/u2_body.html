2Potential energy

A conservative force can be written as the negative gradient of a potential:

F = −dU/dx,   U = −∫F dx

4Work-energy with friction

A block slides down an incline of height h and angle θ with friction μ; the distance along the plane is h/sinθ.

mgh − μmg cosθ (h/sinθ) = ½mv²
v² = 2gh(1 − μ cotθ)
Eg. h = 5 m, θ = 30°, μ = 0.2 → v² = 98.1(1 − 0.346) = 64.1, v = 8.0 m/s.

If the block is pushed up the plane, gravity and friction both do negative work; the initial kinetic energy must exceed mgh + μmg cosθ·d.

5Oblique collisions

Resolve velocities along and perpendicular to the line of impact. Momentum is conserved in both directions; restitution applies only along the line of impact.

m1u1n + m2u2n = m1v1n + m2v2n
e = (v2n − v1n)/(u1n − u2n)
v1t = u1t,   v2t = u2t
Eg. A ball striking a smooth wall at 30° rebounds at 30° with the normal component reversed and the tangential component unchanged; for e = 1, speed is unchanged.

6Work-energy theorem

Use F = ma = m(dv/dt) and dx = v dt:

∫F dx = ∫m v dv
Wnet = ΔK = ½mv2² − ½mv1²
Eg. 2 kg speeds up from 3 m/s to 5 m/s → ΔK = ½(2)(25−9) = 16 J; the net work done is 16 J.

The theorem uses the net work of all forces; work is a scalar and can be positive, negative or zero.

7Momentum and impulse

p = mv,   F = dp/dt
J = ∫F dt = Δp = mv2 − mv1

If the net external force is zero, total momentum is conserved. The impulse is the area under the force-time graph; for a constant force, J = FΔt.

Eg. 0.5 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s and rebounds at 6 m/s: Δp = 0.5(−6−10) = −8 N·s; over 0.02 s, Favg = 400 N.

8Centre of mass

R = (Σmiri)/M,   M = Σmi
V = (Σmivi)/M,   Fext = MaCM

The centre of mass moves as if all mass and all external forces acted there; internal forces cancel in pairs.

Eg. Masses 1 kg at (0,0), 2 kg at (3,0), 3 kg at (0,4): X = 6/6 = 1 m, Y = 12/6 = 2 m; CM = (1, 2) m.

9Satellite energy, escape and vis-viva

Circular: E = ½mv² − GMm/r = −GMm/(2r)
Escape: ½mve² − GMm/R = 0
ve = √(2GM/R) = √2 vcircular
Vis-viva: v² = GM(2/r − 1/a)

The total energy is negative for a bound orbit, zero for a parabolic escape and positive for a hyperbolic trajectory.

Eg. Earth: ve = √(2×9.81×6.37×106) = 11.2 km/s; a circular orbit at the surface would have 7.9 km/s.

10Effective potential

For a central force, the radial motion is equivalent to one-dimensional motion in an effective potential:

Ueff(r) = U(r) + L²/(2mr²)
Feff = −dUeff/dr

The L²/2mr² term is the centrifugal barrier. A circular orbit occurs at a minimum of Ueff: dUeff/dr = 0, d²Ueff/dr² > 0.

Eg. For U = −k/r, the circular radius is r0 = L²/(mk); the orbit is stable because Ueff has a minimum there.

11Binding energy and two-body motion

Binding energy of a uniform sphere:
Ub = 3GM²/(5R)
Two-body relative motion: μ = m1m2/(m1+m2)
T² = 4π²a³/[G(m1+m2)]

The total energy of a bound orbit is negative; the binding energy is the energy needed to separate the masses to infinity. For a two-body system, replace M by m1+m2 and use the relative separation.

Eg. For a binary with m1+m2 = 2Msun and a = 1 AU, T = √(1/2) yr = 0.707 yr.

12Angular momentum

L = r × p,   τ = dL/dt
Rigid body about a fixed axis: L = Iω

If the external torque about an axis is zero, angular momentum about that axis is conserved. This is the basis of Kepler's second law and of spin changes.

Eg. I = 2 kg·m², ω = 5 rad/s → L = 10 kg·m²/s; a torque 4 N·m for 2 s adds 8 kg·m²/s, so ω2 = 9 rad/s.

13Satellite in a circular orbit

GMm/r² = mv²/r
v = √(GM/r),   T = 2π√(r³/GM)

g at the surface: GM = gR², so v = √(gR²/r). Low Earth orbit v ≈ 7.9 km/s; geostationary orbit has r ≈ 42,164 km from Earth's centre (altitude ≈ 35,786 km).

Eg. r = 7,000 km, GM = 3.986×1014 m³/s² → v = 7.54 km/s, T = 97 min.

17Rocket propulsion

For a rocket ejecting mass at speed u relative to the rocket, momentum conservation gives:

m dv/dt = −u dm/dt
Thrust F = −u dm/dt
Δv = u ln(m0/mf)
Eg. m0 = 1000 kg, mf = 200 kg, u = 2000 m/s → Δv = 2000 ln5 = 3219 m/s; if the burn takes 60 s, average thrust ≈ 26.7 kN.

18Conservation of mechanical energy

K1 + U1 = K2 + U2
K1 + U1 + Wnc = K2 + U2

Mechanical energy is conserved when only conservative forces do work. Friction, air resistance and applied forces are non-conservative and change the total energy.

Eg. 2 kg falls 5 m from rest: mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = 9.9 m/s; all potential energy becomes kinetic.

19Torque

τ = r × F,   |τ| = rF sin θ
2D: τz = xFy − yFx

Torque measures the turning effect of a force about a point or axis; it is zero when the line of action passes through the point. A couple is two equal, opposite forces and has moment Fd.

Eg. r = (2, 1) m, F = (3, 4) N: τz = 2(4) − 1(3) = 5 N·m; a force through the origin gives zero torque.

20Central forces and areal velocity

A central force is always directed along the line joining the particle to a fixed centre: F = F(r) r̂. Its torque about the centre is zero, so angular momentum is conserved and the motion is planar.

L = mr²(dθ/dt) = constant
dA/dt = ½r²(dθ/dt) = L/(2m) = constant

This constant areal velocity is Kepler's second law. The particle moves faster near the centre and slower far away.

21Orbit equation for a central force

Put u = 1/r and h = L/m. Newton's second law along the radial direction gives the orbit equation:

d²u/dθ² + u = −F(1/u)/(mh²u²)

For an inverse-square force F = −k/r² = −ku²:

d²u/dθ² + u = k/(mh²)
r = p/(1 + e cos θ),   p = mh²/k = h²/GM

e < 1 ellipse, e = 1 parabola, e > 1 hyperbola. For gravity, k = GMm.

22Gravitational field and potential

g = −GM/r² r̂,   V = −GM/r
U = −GMm/r,   g = −dV/dr

The field is the force per unit mass; the potential is the potential energy per unit mass. Both are negative and tend to zero at infinity. GM = gR² at the surface.

Eg. At r = 2R: g = g0/4 = 2.45 m/s², V = −GM/(2R); the potential energy of 1 kg is half its surface value.

23Geostationary orbit

A geostationary satellite has a period equal to Earth's rotation, T = 86,400 s, and orbits above the equator.

GMm/r² = mω²r,   ω = 2π/T
r = (GM T²/4π²)1/3
Eg. r = 42,164 km from Earth's centre → altitude 35,786 km; orbital speed v = 2πr/T = 3.07 km/s.

The orbit is unique for a given central body and period; a lower orbit has a shorter period, a higher orbit a longer one.
