1Method of joints

Steps: find the support reactions from the whole truss; choose a joint with at most two unknown member forces; draw its FBD; assume all unknown members in tension (arrows away from the joint); solve ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0; move to the next joint.

At every joint:   ΣFx = 0,   ΣFy = 0

A positive result means tension; a negative result means compression. Carry the sign into the next joint, where the member force acts in the opposite direction on the other end.

Eg. At a joint, a 10 kN load acts down and two members at 45° carry it: ΣFy: 2F sin45 = 10 → F = 7.07 kN; the sign tells tension or compression.

Joint selection: start where at most two unknown member forces meet; after solving, the force on the far joint acts in the opposite direction.

2Method of sections

Cut the truss through at most three members whose forces are unknown. Keep one portion, replace the cut members by axial forces (assume tension), and use three equilibrium equations.

Use this method when only a few member forces are needed. The cut may be straight or curved, but it must divide the truss into two separate parts.

Eg. Cut members CD, CB and AB and take the left portion. With reactions known, ΣMC = 0 gives FAB; ΣFy = 0 gives FCB; ΣFx = 0 gives FCD.

Check: the three cut forces must satisfy all three equations; a negative value means compression.

3Angle of repose and friction cone

Angle of repose θr = tan−1 μs: the steepest incline on which a block remains at rest. On an incline, N = W cos θ and friction F = W sin θ; at impending slip, tan θ = μs.

Cone of friction: the rough-surface reaction R = N + F makes an angle φs = tan−1 μs with the normal. For equilibrium, R must lie inside the cone; at impending motion it lies on the cone.

Derivation: on the incline, N = W cos θ, F = W sin θ; at impending slip F = μN → tan θ = μ. The friction cone has half-angle φ = tan−1 μ, since tan φ = F/N = μ.

Eg. μ = 0.4 → θr = 21.8°; at 30° the block slides, at 15° it stays.

4Collar friction

A collar bearing has an annular contact from r1 to r2 and carries an axial load W. The friction torque depends on the pressure assumption.

Uniform pressure:
M = (2/3) μW (r23 − r13) / (r22 − r12)

Uniform wear:
M = (1/2) μW (r1 + r2)

For a flat pivot (r1 = 0): M = (2/3) μW r (uniform pressure) or M = (1/2) μW r (uniform wear).

Eg. W = 10 kN, r1 = 0.1 m, r2 = 0.2 m, μ = 0.2: uniform pressure M = 311 N·m; uniform wear M = 300 N·m.

Derivation: dM = μ p (2πr dr) r. Uniform pressure p = W/[π(r22 − r12)] gives the first formula. Uniform wear p r = C, with W = 2πC(r2 − r1), gives the second.

5Friction: worked examples

FBD→impending direction→F = μN→equilibrium→check
  • Assume the direction of impending motion; friction acts opposite. A negative answer reverses the assumption.
  • Trusses: reactions first, then zero-force members, then joints or a section.
  • Belt: θ in radians and the actual wrap angle; collar: pressure vs wear.
  • Inclines: resolve along and perpendicular to the plane; N = W cos θ only when no other normal force acts.
Eg. 1 Block 200 N, μs = 0.3: maximum static friction 60 N; a 50 N pull leaves it at rest, 70 N starts motion.
Eg. 2 Belt μ = 0.3, θ = π: T2/T1 = 2.57; if T1 = 200 N, T2 = 514 N at impending slip.
Eg. 3 Collar W = 2 kN, r1 = 0.05 m, r2 = 0.15 m, μ = 0.15: uniform wear M = 30 N·m.

Workflow: reactions → zero-force members → choose joints or a section → solve → check one joint or the whole truss.

Eg. To find only the central bottom-chord force, a section through three members gives it in one moment equation; to find every member force, joint-by-joint is usually faster.

Equation count: a section has three equations, so it can solve at most three unknown cut forces directly.

7Belt wrap angle

For an open belt with pulley radii R > r and centre distance C:

sin α = (R − r)/C
θsmall = π − 2α,   θlarge = π + 2α

For a crossed belt: sin α = (R + r)/C and both pulleys have θ = π + 2α. Use the wrap angle of the pulley where slip is being checked; the smaller pulley usually governs.

Eg. R = 0.3 m, r = 0.15 m, C = 1.2 m: open belt sin α = 0.125 → α = 7.18°; θsmall = 165.6° = 2.89 rad, θlarge = 194.4° = 3.39 rad. Crossed belt: sin α = 0.375 → α = 22.0°, θ = 224.1° = 3.91 rad.

8Truss determinacy and stability

For a plane truss, with m members, j joints and r reaction components:

m + r = 2j  → statically determinate (if stable)
m + r > 2j  → statically indeterminate
m + r < 2j  → unstable mechanism

For a general 2D truss, external determinacy usually means r = 3 (e.g. pin + roller). Internal stability needs triangles; a quadrilateral panel without a diagonal is a mechanism.

Eg. m = 5, r = 3, j = 4 → 8 = 8, determinate.
Eg. m=6, j=5, r=3: 9 < 10 → unstable; add a member. m=7: 10 = 10 → determinate. m=8: 11 > 10 → indeterminate.

Internal stability: triangulate every panel; a rectangular panel without a diagonal is a mechanism even if the count matches.

9Belt friction

Flat belt: at impending slip toward the tight side, T2/T1 = eμθ, where T2 is the tight tension, T1 the slack tension, and θ the wrap angle in radians. Equivalently, ln(T2/T1) = μθ.

V-belt: T2/T1 = eμθ/sin α, where α is the half groove angle. For a flat belt, α = 90° and the formula reduces to the flat-belt case.

Eg. μ = 0.3, θ = π → T2/T1 = e0.942 = 2.57.

Derivation: over a small angle dθ, the tension change dT is balanced by friction μN; with N ≈ T dθ, dT/T = μ dθ. Integrate from T1 to T2: ln(T2/T1) = μθ.

Eg. μ = 0.25, θ = 2.5 rad → T2/T1 = e0.625 = 1.87.

10Truss example: triangular truss

Span L, height h, pin at A, roller at B, load P downward at apex C. By symmetry:

Ax = 0,   Ay = By = P/2
At C: 2F sin θ = P
FAC = FBC = P/(2 sin θ)   (compression)
FAB = P/(2 tan θ)   (tension)

θ is the angle each rafter makes with the horizontal. At A, the compression in AC pushes down-left; the bottom chord AB pulls right to balance it.

Eg. P = 20 kN, θ = 45°: FAC = FBC = 20/(2 sin45) = 14.1 kN compression; FAB = 20/(2 tan45) = 10 kN tension.

Check at A: vertical 14.1 sin45 = 10 kN balances Ay; horizontal 14.1 cos45 = 10 kN balances FAB.

11Belt drive: power

The power transmitted by a belt is the difference in tension times the belt speed:

P = (T2 − T1) v,   v = πDN/60

T2 is the tight side, T1 the slack side, D the pulley diameter, and N the speed in rpm. At impending slip, T2/T1 = eμθ.

Eg. D = 0.5 m, N = 600 rpm → v = 15.7 m/s; if T2 − T1 = 500 N, P = 7.85 kW.
Eg. T2 = 800 N, T1 = 300 N, v = 12 m/s → P = 6 kW. Torque T = (T2 − T1)r; with D = 0.4 m, N ≈ 573 rpm gives v ≈ 12 m/s.

12Plane trusses: assumptions

A plane truss is a framework of straight members pin-connected at joints, loaded only at the joints. Its weight is neglected or lumped at the joints, and the pins are frictionless.

Under these assumptions every member is a two-force member: the force is axial, either tension (pulls the joint, arrow away) or compression (pushes the joint, arrow toward).

Two-force member proof: if forces act only at A and B, ΣMA = 0 requires the line of action of FB to pass through A; ΣF = 0 then gives FA = −FB. The member force is axial and collinear with AB.

Eg. A bar pinned at both ends with no intermediate load is a two-force member; its force acts along AB, in tension or compression.

13Zero-force members

  • Two non-collinear members at an unloaded joint → both member forces are zero.
  • Three members at an unloaded joint, with two collinear → the third member is zero.

Identify these first: they simplify the joint equations and reduce the number of unknowns. Remove their forces, not necessarily the members themselves.

Why: at an unloaded joint, ΣF = 0 with two non-collinear members can only be satisfied by zero forces; with two collinear members and a third, the collinear pair balances and the third is zero.

Eg. A T-joint with no external load has the vertical member as a zero-force member; the two collinear members carry equal and opposite forces.

14Friction laws

Static friction adjusts up to a maximum: Fs ≤ μsN. At impending motion, Fs = μsN. Once sliding starts, Fk = μkN, and usually μs > μk.

StateFriction forceMotion
Static, no impending slipF < μsNnone
Impending slipF = μsNabout to start
SlidingF = μkNrelative motion

Friction opposes the impending or relative motion. Coulomb's law assumes dry surfaces and a coefficient independent of the contact area.

Friction angle: at impending motion F = μN, so the resultant reaction makes φ = tan−1 μ with the normal. On an incline at the angle of repose, tan θ = μ.

Eg. μs = 0.35 → θr = φ = 19.3°; on a 25° incline, tan 25° = 0.466 > 0.35, so the block slides.

15Friction on an incline with a force

For a block of weight W on an incline θ, N = W cos θ. With a force P parallel to the plane:

Impending up:   P = W sin θ + μW cos θ
Impending down:   P = W sin θ − μW cos θ

At impending up, friction acts down the plane; at impending down, it acts up the plane. If P acts at angle α to the plane, resolve it into P cos α along the plane and P sin α perpendicular to it; include P sin α in the normal equation with the correct sign (a pull away from the plane reduces N).

Eg. W = 500 N, θ = 20°, μ = 0.25. Impending up: P = 171 + 117 = 288 N. Impending down: P = 171 − 117 = 54 N.
